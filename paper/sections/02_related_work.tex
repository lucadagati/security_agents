\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We group prior work into five strands. The gap this paper occupies is
their combination: autonomous agents, a cybersecurity environment,
repeated interaction, strategy adaptation, and co-evolution.

\subsection{LLM Agents and Tool Use}
ReAct-style loops couple reasoning with acting through external
tools~\cite{yao2023react,schick2023toolformer}. Systems such as AutoGen,
generative-agent societies and commercial GPT agents show that multiple
language-model instances can plan, delegate and persist
memory~\cite{wu2023autogen,park2023generative,xi2023rise,shinn2023reflexion}.
We adopt a deliberately simple loop (goal, observation, memory, tool
selection) so that strategy variation can be attributed to adaptation
level rather than to planner architecture.

\subsection{Security of Agentic Systems}
Indirect prompt injection~\cite{greshake2023not}, transferable
jailbreaks~\cite{zou2023universal} and systematic red
teaming~\cite{perez2022red} established that language models can be
steered into unintended actions. For \emph{agents} the attack surface
grows: tool misuse, memory poisoning and environment injection are now
benchmarked by AgentDojo, InjecAgent and
AgentPoison~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,zhan2024injecagent,chen2024agentpoison}.
Multi-agent studies further document collusion and steganographic
coordination~\cite{abdelnabi2024cooperation}. These evaluations are
mostly attacker-centric or one-shot. They do not ask what happens when
the defender is an equally autonomous learner that remembers previous
episodes.

\subsection{Autonomous Cyber Defence and Cyber Ranges}
High-fidelity ranges and gyms---CybORG/CAGE, CyberBattleSim, FARLAND---let
reinforcement-learning defenders operate on emulated
networks~\cite{standen2021cyborg,microsoft2021cyberbattlesim,molina2021network}.
MITRE ATT\&CK provides the vocabulary for adversary
behaviour~\cite{strom2018attck}. These environments typically train a
defender against a scripted or slowly varying attacker, or an attacker
against a fixed defender. Co-evolution of \emph{both} populations of
LLM-based agents, with persistent memory and an explicit emergence
criterion, is not their object of study.

\subsection{Security Games and Multi-Agent Learning}
Stackelberg security games allocate defensive resources against a best
responding attacker~\cite{tambe2011security}. Multi-agent reinforcement
learning studies cooperation and competition under partial
observability~\cite{lowe2017multi,foerster2018counterfactual,kaelbling1998planning}.
The present work sits closer to the latter: both sides learn, neither is
assumed to hold a closed-form best response, and the state is a cyber
environment rather than an abstract matrix game.

\subsection{Evolutionary Computation and Arms Races}
Co-evolution as a machine-learning method dates at least to Hillis'
sorting networks and to competitive fitness sharing~\cite{hillis1990co,rosin1997new}.
Evolutionary game theory supplies the language of replicator dynamics
and evolutionarily stable strategies~\cite{hofbauer2003evolutionary,weibull1995evolutionary}.
We do not run a genetic algorithm on agent weights. We study
\emph{strategic} co-evolution: policies change because agents remember
outcomes and alter subsequent tool choices. The analogy is the security
arms race, not neuroevolution.

\subsection{Research Gap}
Table~\ref{tab:gap} summarises the gap. Single-agent security omits
strategic interaction. Agent-to-agent protocol attacks often freeze the
adversary. Multi-agent collusion studies rarely last long enough for
cross-episode learning. Autonomous defence evaluates the defender.
Evolutionary AI rarely uses a cyber environment. The missing combination
is the one this paper constructs.
