\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This work lies at the intersection of agentic language models, agent
security, autonomous cyber defence, multi-agent learning and competitive
co-evolution. Importantly, prior research has already trained or evolved
attackers and defenders together. Our claim is therefore not that
co-evolution in cybersecurity is new, but that its evaluation should make
both sides' adaptation and memory regimes explicit and should measure the
resulting strategy dynamics over repeated encounters. Table~\ref{tab:gap}
summarises this positioning.

\paragraph{Language-model agents, tools and memory.}
ReAct and Toolformer established influential patterns for coupling language
models to external actions and APIs~\cite{yao2023react,schick2023toolformer}.
Reflexion showed that linguistic feedback stored across trials can alter later
decisions without updating model weights~\cite{shinn2023reflexion}. Generative
Agents, CAMEL and AutoGen extended this design space to persistent memory,
role-conditioned interaction and multi-agent orchestration
~\cite{park2023generative,li2023camel,wu2023autogen}. Surveys and broad
benchmarks organise these capabilities and expose persistent weaknesses in
long-horizon decision making~\cite{xi2025rise,liu2024agentbench}. This line of
work supplies the architectural ingredients used here, but generally measures
task completion while the environment and other agents follow a fixed
protocol. We instead treat memory regime and opponent adaptation as
independent experimental factors.

\paragraph{Security evaluation of agentic systems.}
Model-level red teaming and transferable jailbreaks demonstrate failures of
aligned language models~\cite{perez2022red,zou2023universal}. Indirect prompt
injection and its formal benchmarking show how untrusted context can redirect
tool-integrated applications~\cite{greshake2023not,liu2024formalizing}.
ToolEmu evaluates consequential actions in an LM-emulated sandbox
~\cite{ruan2024toolemu}, while AgentDojo, InjecAgent and AgentPoison cover tool
misuse, environment injection and poisoning of long-term memory or retrieval
stores~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,zhan2024injecagent,chen2024agentpoison}.
Multi-agent work additionally studies adversarial negotiation and covert
steganographic collusion~\cite{abdelnabi2024cooperation,motwani2024secret}.
These studies greatly expand the relevant attack surface. Even when attacks
are generated adaptively, however, the principal benchmark unit is normally a
task or attack--defence cell rather than a long sequence in which both roles
retain experience and change policy in response to one another.

\paragraph{Autonomous cyber agents and ranges.}
CybORG combines simulated and emulated cyber environments behind a common
agent interface~\cite{standen2021cyborg}; CyberBattleSim has also been extended
to learn red and blue policies jointly~\cite{kunz2022multiagent}. NASim frames
autonomous penetration testing as sequential decision making
~\cite{schwartz2019autonomous}, whereas Network Defense Is Not a Game argues
that evaluation should span distributions of drifting network conditions
~\cite{molina2021network}. Two-player, knowledge-guided reinforcement learning
further demonstrates simultaneous attacker--defender improvement in a cyber
simulator~\cite{piplai2022knowledge}. MITRE ATT\&CK supplies the empirical
vocabulary used to describe adversary tactics and techniques
~\cite{strom2020attck}. These platforms and algorithms are the closest
environment-side foundations for our work. Their usual endpoints are return,
success, convergence or transfer of a learned policy; cross-episode memory
regimes and trajectory-level measures of reciprocal strategy change are not
their primary experimental variables.

\paragraph{Cybersecurity co-evolution.}
There is a direct lineage of competitive evolutionary cybersecurity research.
CANDLES co-evolves rule-based attacker and defender populations
~\cite{rush2015candles}; later work co-evolves network segmentation and attack
strategies~\cite{hemberg2018adversarial}. Turner et al. compare multi-agent
reinforcement learning and co-evolution for penetration testing
~\cite{turner2022analyzing}, and Shashkov et al. compare reinforcement
learning, evolutionary strategies and tree search under simultaneous
attacker--defender training~\cite{shashkov2023adversarial}. Most recently,
Hemberg et al. study one-sided versus two-sided evolution of cyber-agent teams
and include an LLM-supported mutation operator~\cite{hemberg2025evolutionary}.
These papers are our closest methodological predecessors. They optimise
parameterised controllers or populations; our complementary focus is on
behavioural adaptation induced by declared memory regimes, including cases in
which the LLM itself is the acting policy, and on longitudinal indicators such
as strategy signatures and co-evolutionary pressure.

The broader conceptual basis comes from competitive co-evolution and
evolutionary games. Hillis demonstrated that co-evolving adversaries can
improve search, and Rosin and Belew introduced competitive fitness sharing and
shared sampling~\cite{hillis1990co,rosin1997new}. Replicator dynamics and
evolutionarily stable strategies formalise population-level change
~\cite{hofbauer1998evolutionary,weibull1995evolutionary}. We use
``co-evolution'' descriptively for reciprocal policy change rather than to
claim that model weights are evolved.

\paragraph{Security games and multi-agent learning.}
Stackelberg security games model defensive commitment against a best-responding
attacker~\cite{tambe2011security,sinha2018stackelberg}. POMDPs formalise
decision making under hidden state~\cite{kaelbling1998planning}, while modern
multi-agent actor--critic methods address mixed competition, non-stationarity
and decentralised execution~\cite{lowe2017multi,foerster2018counterfactual}.
These theories motivate our partially observable repeated game, but do not by
themselves provide cyber semantics or a protocol for distinguishing static,
episodic and persistent opponents. Our evaluation therefore reports outcome
metrics together with process metrics that reveal whether strategies are
changing, converging or entering an arms race.

The resulting gap is narrow but consequential: existing cyber co-evolution
shows that learning on both sides matters, while agentic-security benchmarks
show that tools, memory and inter-agent messages create new failure modes. We
connect these lines by making adaptation a controlled variable for both roles
and by measuring not only who wins, but how their strategies change across
repeated cyber interaction.
