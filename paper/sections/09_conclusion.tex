\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work introduced a co-evolutionary framework for studying
cybersecurity in autonomous multi-agent systems, where attacker and
defender agents repeatedly interact, learn from previous encounters,
and adapt their strategies. The scientific object is not a new
benchmark as such, but the empirical study of offensive and defensive
behaviours, arms races, coalitions and interaction-induced risks that
cannot be observed when agents are evaluated in isolation.

A one-versus-one adaptation ladder is already enough to see the
phenomenon. Static attackers are detected; adaptive attackers wait,
evade, and succeed more often; persistent populations keep moving
through strategy space and produce behaviours---patience, delayed
attack, deception---that no script named. The next step is to scale
the same instrument: communication and coalitions (E3--E7), dynamic
topology (E8), LLM policies on local GPUs (B3--B5), and
high-fidelity validation on the Kubernetes range.

Longer term, the same loop can be pointed at prediction (detect a
strategy shift before the next compromise), at defensive co-evolution
as a first-class training regime, at agent societies in which trust
and withholding are themselves security-relevant, and at
cyber-physical settings where an AI agent, a cyber system, a physical
plant and other agents close the loop. In all of those settings the
methodological moral is the same: \emph{do not freeze the opponent}.
